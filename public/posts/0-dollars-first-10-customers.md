If you had a working product, zero marketing budget, no audience, and no investors—how would you get your first 10 customers? 

It is the most universal founder pain point. You've spent weeks or months building a product you *think* people want, but when it comes time to launch, there's just crickets. 

Recently, a founder asked this exact question to a community of experienced entrepreneurs. The thread blew up with over 40 seasoned founders weighing in. I analyzed the top responses, and the results were eye-opening. 

It turns out, a lot of the standard marketing advice you read online is just cope. But hidden in the trenches were brutal, contrarian truths about what *actually* works when your budget is exactly $0.

Here is the breakdown of what works, what scales, and the one harsh reality most technical founders want to avoid.

## The Myth: "Build It and They Will Come"

The biggest trap technical founders fall into is building in a vacuum. You assume that if you write clean code and launch a bug-free product, the market will magically find you. 

As one founder put it: *"How do you know you have a product worth buying if you have no audience? Building a product in a vacuum is not usually a winning strategy."*

If you have $0, your first marketing step shouldn't happen after the product is built. It should happen before you write a single line of code. Co-creating a product with pilot customers already seeds your audience.

## Bucket 1: Tactics That Don't Scale (Do These First)

Paul Graham famously said, "Do things that don't scale." When you have $0, you can't rely on automated funnels. You are in the learning and feedback stage. Your goal isn't mass marketing; it's finding Product-Market Fit (PMF).

Here are the manual, unscalable tactics that actually drive those first 10 sales:

### 1. Highly Targeted Cold Outreach (Not Spam)
Cold calling and cold DMs still work, but with a massive caveat. *Stop increasing your volume and start increasing your relevance.* 

Ten conversations with people who actively experience the problem you're solving will teach you more than a hundred random calls. Build a highly targeted list. 
*   **LinkedIn DMs:** Reach out directly to people actively asking about or experiencing the problem.
*   **The "Smile and Dial" Method:** Pick up the phone and call local businesses if that's your target market. It sucks, it takes time, but it gets a sale.

### 2. Manual Community Engagement (The 15-Minute Rule)
One founder shared a highly effective, free routine: Spend 15-30 minutes a day across Reddit, Facebook Groups, Threads, and LinkedIn. 
*   Post, comment, and reply. 
*   Be friendly, not pushy. 
*   **No selling unless someone explicitly asks.**

Offer to solve one concrete problem manually for a narrow customer type where they already hang out.

## Bucket 2: Tactics That Scale (Do These For The Future)

While manual outreach gets your first 10 customers, you need a strategy that gets you to 1,000 without burning out. This is where you leverage your technical skills to build growth engines that compound over time.

### Programmatic SEO and Content
If you have zero dollars but can code, use your skills to build programmatic SEO assets. 

One founder shared a brilliant example: For their rhyming app, they programmatically created a rhyming dictionary with 5.8 million words across 22 languages. This created millions of indexable pages. After a few months, it hit 500k daily search impressions and drove 100 new users a day—all organically.

Build free tools, calculators, or directories that naturally drive traffic to your main product.

## The Contrarian Truth: Talk To People Manually

If we strip away the noise, almost every successful strategy for a $0 budget boils down to one uncomfortable truth: **You have to manually talk to people before you build anything.**

Stop hiding behind generic social media posts or tweaking your landing page for the 50th time. Pick one narrow customer type, find where they ask for help, and talk to them. 

You do what doesn't scale. You manually reach out to people you believe are your market. You build as you speak to your audience. Ultimately, you need product-market fit—it's the only thing that matters.
